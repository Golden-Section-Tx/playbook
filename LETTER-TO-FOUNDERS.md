# A Letter to Founders

My kids love the stories in *The Book of Virtues*, and "The King and His Hawk"
is a family favorite. James Baldwin wrote it, and the king in it is Genghis
Khan.

He is out hunting with his favorite hawk, and after a long day he is tired and
thirsty, and he finds a trickle of water running off a cliff from some pool
higher up. He holds his cup under it and fills it one drop at a time. When it is
full he raises it to drink, and the hawk swoops down and knocks it out of his
hand. He fills it again, and the hawk spills it again.

The third time, he keeps his sword drawn behind his back, and when the hawk
swoops he cuts the bird in two. The cup goes onto the rocks and shatters. So he
climbs the cliff to drink from the pool itself, and at the top edge he finds a
dead snake lying in the water. The pool is poisoned, and the hawk had been
protecting him the whole time. He just could not see it.

It is a story about friendship and loyalty and the value of someone whose view
is wider than your own. All of us know intuitively that advice is worth having,
and all of us bristle at receiving it. We are like the king. We see the world
from our own frame and we are mostly interested in satisfying our own definition
of right.

But there is a part of that story nobody preaches. The king had no way to know.
The hawk looked exactly like a nuisance right up until he stood at the top of
that cliff, and the difference only became visible after the bird was dead. That
is the actual problem you have. You are not short of interruptions. You have a
board and an investor and a lead engineer and a spouse and a man you met at a
conference, they contradict each other, and most of them are wrong. So how do
you tell a hawk from a nuisance?

I think the difference is that a hawk has seen this pool before. Not an opinion
about your business, but a pattern out of other people's businesses that happens
to fit yours. Golden Section has operated alongside vertical SaaS founders for
more than a decade and watched over 400 companies make the same mistakes in
roughly the same order, and the order is the useful part. The danger in front of
you is usually the one that has already killed companies at your stage, and it
is knowable before it arrives.

When I was running Prognosis, a healthcare software company, the business was
constantly out of money and every encounter was existential. The customer saying
yes was paramount. One of the fatal flaws I narrowly avoided in that season was
our account management motion. We had none. Customers got operational engagement
from the service desk and nothing above it, no quarterly business review and no
strategic conversation of any kind.

It felt like too heavy a lift at the time, and we had other fires burning, and I
could not see how a scheduled quarterly meeting with a customer who already had
my cell number was going to change anything. Besides, there were customers I did
not want to go sit with. You have your own list. An advisor insisted, and we did
it anyway.

I felt like the king accepting the hawk's interruptions as law. It felt
ridiculous. I did not believe it would do anything except stress resources
that were already thin. But slowly, customer by customer, satisfaction
improved. Within two quarters an upsell motion that had been stuck was halfway
through re-contracting the whole customer base at more than 25% annual uplift
in contracted revenue. Relationships that had been raw from a pattern of
conflict in every meeting got softer, and the cortisol spike that used to fire
off a customer's name on the caller ID went away. The intervention I had no
capacity for spilled into excess capacity everywhere, and into new business.

There are hundreds of stories like that one. Each one carries some of the weight
of where I sit today. Each one started as an intervention that felt unintuitive,
or incorrect, or dangerous.

And plenty of advice was simply wrong. We engaged a third-party audit on the
insistence of that same advisor, and it would "eliminate financial pushback in
exit diligence," he told me. It cost us $45,000. The pushback came anyway,
question for question, and I have never been able to point at a dollar that
audit saved us. A hawk that has seen one pool will tell you about that pool
whether or not you are standing near it.

So weigh it. What is in this repository is our collective experience working
with founders: 161 mistakes we have watched companies make, and the 63 plays
that prevent them. Will reading a list of mistakes keep you from making them? I
don't think so, not on its own. I think it works the way the hawk worked, by
getting between you and the cup at the moment you are most sure of yourself.

Start with [MISTAKES.md](MISTAKES.md) and read it end to end, which takes about
twenty minutes. You will recognize three of them as things happening inside your
company right now, and those three are your hawk. Then run the
[plays](plays/README.md) attached to them, beginning with the one you are most
inclined to skip.

We hope this meets you on your journey and blesses you and your team. And we
hope the interventions that enrage you do not end with you killing the hawk.

Dougal Cameron
Golden Section, August 2026
