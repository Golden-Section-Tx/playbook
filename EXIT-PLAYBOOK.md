# The Exit Playbook

## Introduction

A ColdFusion memory leak was the small issue that kept popping up at the worst
time, but never seemed big enough or pervasive enough to deserve dedicated
resources. Every entrepreneur has a list of those small things that evade
resolution. For me, running Prognosis, the memory leak was that thing.

It made our software run slow and get trapped in load cycles, seemingly when
clinical users needed it most. It also seemed to happen at one hospital more
than anywhere else. The team there had a lot of patience with us, but over the
years it wore thin. We led them on a wild goose chase of fixes: more internet
bandwidth, faster PCs, hard-wired fiber between buildings, and more. Nothing
solved it.

Eventually the team concluded that our software must be the problem. We
investigated and found a shortcut. The ColdFusion engine had to be restarted
inside the application, because there was a memory leak. Finding and fixing
the root cause was a large, unscoped job. Fixing the symptom when it popped up
was known and took an hour or so. So that is what we did.

Successful exits require intentionality. Or so we are told. Too often, though,
founders reach the exit feeling like they tripped uphill and arrived covered
in mud, rather than crossing the finish line with confidence. We have been
there. More importantly, we have seen it done right and done wrong. This
playbook is our contribution to your finish line. It is the way you *ought* to
finish, with no shade or guilt if you happen to finish differently.

When it came time to sell Prognosis, a few years after that first memory leak,
the process seemed to be going to plan. The buyer had revenue synergies with
us, and we were talking about numbers that seemed to make everything work. I
could tell there was more to it than the revenue synergies, but I did not
really care. The sight of the finish line was more appealing than maximizing
the return.

The longing to finish overwhelmed me, and it impaired my ability to finish
well. That is exactly why this playbook exists. Every company carries its own
memory leak toward the exit: the small, known, never-quite-urgent problem that
was always patched and never fixed. And every founder meets the same longing
at the end. There are things you need to be doing at mile 2 so that at mile 24
you can lock in and run through the finish line with poise.

Dougal Cameron

## The sequence

Eight plays, in the order a company meets them. The first defines what the end
has to look like. The next three make the company worth buying and prove it.
The last four prepare and run the sale.

| # | Play | What it produces | Start it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 72 | [Meaningful Exit Plan](plays/executive/saas-meaningful-exit-plan.md) | Your Number with its Floor, Enough and Temptation, your weighted priorities, the exit structure and buyer archetype, and the gap to what that buyer underwrites | Now, whatever your stage |
| 75 | [Exit Roadmap](plays/executive/saas-exit-roadmap.md) | Every gap turned into a workstream, sequenced by lead time, with owners, quarterly priorities and a dated exit window | Right after the Meaningful Exit Plan |
| 76 | [Exit Data Room](plays/executive/saas-exit-data-room.md) | A data room built around the questions a buyer asks, owned folder by folder, reviewed on a cadence, and ready for a quality of earnings review | Years before a sale, and never finished |
| 77 | [Founder Independence (The Vacation Test)](plays/executive/saas-founder-independence.md) | A company that sells, serves and decides without you, proven by 30-day, 90-day and six-month tests | As soon as the roadmap names it, which is usually first |
| 78 | [Pre-Sale Value Levers](plays/executive/saas-pre-sale-value-levers.md) | Four to six value levers moved in run-rate, a sell-side QoE, and the tax, cap table and contract debris cleared | 12 to 18 months before going to market |

## Why this order

**Define the end first.** The Meaningful Exit Plan is the cheapest play in the
sequence and the one every other decision depends on. The first honest answer
to what the end should look like is the one you will never get cheaper, and
every later play assumes you have it.

**Then make the company worth buying.** The Exit Roadmap, the Exit Data Room
and Founder Independence are slow, quiet work that happens quarter after
quarter, and most of the value in an exit is created here. Structural gaps
found here are work. Found in diligence, they are a discount.

**Then run the sale.** Pre-Sale Value Levers and Selecting an Investment Banker
open the final stretch; Running the Company During a Sale and Negotiating the
Exit carry it to the end. None of them work well if the quiet work was skipped.
They turn readiness into a price; they cannot manufacture readiness in a year.

## How to use it

Find the row that describes you. Start there, and go back for anything upstream
you skipped, because a later play assumes the earlier ones were done.

**Five or more years from an exit.** Complete the Meaningful Exit Plan this
year and re-run it every year. Start the data room now as a habit, not a
project. Put founder independence on the roadmap early; the percentage of new
revenue you personally close is the slowest number in this playbook to move.
Do not talk to bankers yet.

**Two to three years out.** The roadmap becomes the spine of your quarterly
priorities. Work the structural gaps first: customer concentration, founder
dependency, a contract book that does not survive a change of control, revenue
from a business the buyer will not pay for. Run the 30-day and 90-day vacation
tests. Move the data room from a folder structure to something a buyer's
accountant could work from. Start testing pricing.

**Twelve to eighteen months out.** Choose your four to six value levers and
get them into run-rate where a buyer will see them hold for at least two
quarters. Commission a sell-side quality of earnings review and clear the
debris list. Open informal banker conversations and listen hardest to the ones
who tell you what to fix. Re-base the plan to a forecast you will beat. Size
the retention pool. Write your walk-away while you are still rested.

**In process.** Run the company through a named operator and a weekly exec
meeting that never skips. Answer diligence from one tracker and the data room.
Model every offer as cash at close, cash within two years, and contingent
value. Negotiate the money terms before exclusivity, keep a second bidder
warm, expect the re-trade, and hold the line you drew. If you are here and
the Meaningful Exit Plan does not exist, stop and do it this week. It takes
an hour, and every decision left in the process depends on it.
